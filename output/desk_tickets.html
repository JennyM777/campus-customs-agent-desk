Reflection

Written after running all three tickets through the real team against gpt-6-luna and approving both resulting payments (see the Cash tab and output/resolved_tickets.json).

1. How we evaluate agent performance, per ticket

We judge each agent run on four things: (a) factual correctness — did every number/date/status it reported trace back to a real MCP tool call, with nothing invented; (b) decision quality — did it correctly identify blockers (stockouts, unpaid invoices, affordability) rather than papering over them; (c) guardrail adherence — did it stop short of executing a payment, going negative on cash, or confirming something uncertain to a customer; and (d) delegation discipline — did the Boss call only the specialists actually needed, or fan out needlessly.

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee
Passes on all four counts. Inventory correctly reported size S at exactly 0 (matching inventory), Accounting correctly flagged invoice 501 as overdue against desk.date_today (2026-08-31 vs. due 2026-08-28) rather than assuming the real-world date, and it proposed — but did not execute — an $840 payment. Customer Service's reply to Tauhid Zaman didn't promise a restock date it didn't have, which is exactly the "don't invent facts" rule in its prompt working as intended.
Ticket 102 — Rent due
Passes. Facilities correctly computed "2 days out, not overdue" (next_due 2026-09-02 minus desk date 2026-08-31) instead of treating the email's own claim ("due in 2 days") as ground truth — it re-derived the number itself, which is exactly why desk.date_today is the enforced anchor rather than trusting ticket text. It also checked the $2,400 rent against the $3,400 balance before drafting the PaymentRequest, correctly finding no shortfall.
Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount
Passes, and is the most interesting case: Inventory correctly computed the exact shortfall (20 requested − 8 on hand = 12 short) instead of just saying "not enough," and Accounting explicitly refused to evaluate the discount ("the ticket provides no quoted discount amount... affordability cannot be checked") rather than guessing a number. That refusal is good behavior, not a failure — it's the "no agent invents data" rule holding under a genuinely ambiguous request.

2. Actual vs. Expected, ticket by ticket

The single biggest pattern across all three tickets: the Boss delegated to more specialists than planned, every time — and the specific extra specialist was always Customer Service.

Ticket 101
Planned: 1 delegation (Accounting only). Actual: 3 (Accounting, Inventory, Customer Service). The plan assumed get_ticket_details's bundled inventory data meant Inventory didn't need to be consulted separately — the real Boss consulted it anyway, for a vendor recommendation the bundled data doesn't include. Human approval: the $840 invoice-501 payment was drafted during the run and approved afterward by Jenny Meng, bringing checking from $3,400 to $2,560.
Ticket 102
Planned: 1 delegation (Facilities only). Actual: 2 (Facilities, Customer Service) — the plan's own "Not delegated to" list had explicitly predicted no Customer Service call here, which turned out wrong. Human approval: the $2,400 rent payment was drafted, then approved afterward, bringing checking from $2,560 to $160.
Ticket 103
Planned: 1 delegation (Inventory only). Actual: 3 (Inventory, Accounting, Customer Service) — same pattern as 101. Human approval: no new payment was drafted for this ticket specifically; it re-surfaced the already-pending invoice 501, which was approved once under ticket 101 and counted there.

In every case the plan correctly identified which specialist mattered most for the core decision — it just under-predicted that the Boss would also loop in Customer Service as a matter of course, rather than holding the reply back. That's a reasonable, low-risk divergence: it makes the team more consistently customer-facing than planned, not less safe.

3. What would have been simpler as a single agent with tools

Honestly, a good deal of this would have been simpler as one agent holding all 9 MCP tools. All three tickets ended up calling 3–4 specialists' worth of tools anyway (just split across agent boundaries), so a single agent would have made the same tool calls with less overhead: no `delegate_to_*` round-trips, no re-serializing a sub-agent's Pydantic output back into a string for the Boss to re-read, and one combined `final_summary` instead of stitching together four separate structured outputs. For a shop this small — three tickets, one vendor relationship, one lease — the "who do I even ask" problem that justifies a Boss/specialist split barely exists yet.

Where the multi-agent structure earns its keep is exactly what it's for: enforcing different rules per domain without one giant prompt trying to hold all of them at once. A single agent's system prompt would need to simultaneously remember "never execute a payment," "check unpaid invoices before recommending a vendor," "never let cash go negative," and "don't invent a customer-facing promise" — all at once, for every ticket, regardless of relevance. Splitting them lets each prompt stay short and let the tool allowlist (enforced in code via `.filtered()`, not just instruction) do some of the enforcement mechanically. That's a real advantage once the shop (or the rule set) grows past what one prompt can hold reliably — just not yet decisively proven at this scale.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with our existing toolset

A. A second vendor's invoice comes due the same week as rent — and this time the math doesn't work

Suppose a new ticket arrives: Elm City Gifts (vendor 2 in our vendors table) sends an invoice for, say, $1,200 for a mug restock, due the same week lease 1's rent ($2,400) is due — but this time it lands after we've already paid out invoice 501 and rent, so checking sits at our actual post-Problem-9 balance of $160.00. Walking through it with our real tools: the Boss calls get_ticket_details, sees an invoice_id pointing at a new row in invoices with vendor_id=2, and delegates to Accounting. Accounting calls get_invoice (amount, due_date, vendor_name), get_desk_today (still 2026-08-31), and get_cash_account — and finds $1,200 against a $160.00 balance. This is exactly the "never let cash go negative" guardrail from backend/prompts/accounting.md firing for real: Accounting reports the shortfall as a blocker and refuses to draft a payable PaymentRequest, rather than proposing one it can't afford. No new tool is needed — this is precisely why get_cash_account exists, and our real run already proved the checking balance tracks correctly across sequential approvals.

B. A customer asks for a size we're out of, but a close substitute exists

This is a direct extension of ticket 101, which our real run already half-answered: CC-TEE-WHITE size S is 0 on hand, but the same check_inventory call that told us that also returned M=5, L=3, XL=2 in one response — it's not a single-size lookup, it returns the whole sku's breakdown by design. A new ticket like "do you have the Bulldog tee in a size S or close to it?" needs no new MCP tool: Inventory already has the full stock-by-size list in hand from a single check_inventory("CC-TEE-WHITE") call, and its prompt already instructs it to report every size, not just the one requested. The only change needed is a Boss/Customer-Service prompt tweak to actively suggest the nearest in-stock size (M, in this case) instead of just reporting the shortfall — a prompt change, not a new capability.

C. "Before we approve anything else this week, can we afford invoice 501 and rent together?"

This is literally the question a human should have asked before Problem 9's two separate approvals — and it's answerable today only because we happened to approve sequentially and check the balance in between ($3,400 → $2,560 → $160.00). If both had been proposed in the same ticket batch, Accounting and Facilities would each independently confirm their own $840 and $2,400 payments fit under $3,400 — both true in isolation — without either one knowing the combined total is $3,240, just $160 under the actual starting balance. No new tool is needed (both agents already call get_cash_account), but the Boss's prompt (backend/prompts/boss.md) would need an explicit instruction to sum every specialist's proposed PaymentRequest.amount against one shared balance snapshot before presenting them together, rather than trusting two independently-affordable answers to also be jointly affordable.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve with our current tools

A. "We keep running out of CC-HOOD-NAVY size M — should we raise the standing reorder quantity?"

Ticket 103 showed us the symptom (8 on hand against a 20-unit request) but our schema has no memory of why — there's no sales or order-history table, only a frozen snapshot of current inventory.qty. We can't tell if size M always runs low or if this was a one-off spike. New table: sales(id, sku, size, qty, sold_at). New MCP tool: get_sales_history(sku, size=None) added to mcp_server/server.py, returning rows from that table the same way check_inventory reads inventory. No new agent needed — this extends Inventory's existing remit; its prompt would just gain a line about checking sell-through rate before recommending a reorder quantity, not just a lead time.

B. "Tauhid wants to return last month's hoodie for a refund, not reorder a tee"

Our entire financial model (payments, /api/payments/approve, every PaymentRequest in backend/models.py) is one-directional: money only ever leaves checking toward a vendor or landlord. A refund is money leaving checking toward a customer — a case our kind: Literal["invoice", "rent"] payment type doesn't even have a slot for, and ticket type has no "return" category either. New table: returns(id, original_sku, size, qty, refund_amount, reason, status). New MCP tool: get_return_eligibility(sku) or similar, plus extending payments.kind to accept "refund". New agent role: a Returns agent (or Customer Service's remit expanded with care, since over-trusting a customer's return claim is a real risk) that drafts a RefundRequest — still human-approved, same pattern as today's PaymentRequest, but flowing money the opposite direction.

C. "Ask Bulldog Print Co for a rush 2-day turnaround on the next CC-TEE-WHITE reprint, even if it costs more"

Every ticket we ran treated Bulldog Print Co's 5-day lead_days in vendors as a fixed fact to report, never something to negotiate. There's no outbound channel to a vendor at all in this system — no email/API integration, and no agent is authorized to make or imply a commitment on the shop's behalf. New tool: an outbound draft_vendor_request(vendor_id, request_text) MCP tool that writes a proposal for human review (mirroring how PaymentRequest is drafted, not sent, until approved) rather than actually emailing the vendor. New agent role: a Vendor Relations agent, separate from Inventory — negotiating terms and prices is a materially different (and riskier) skill than checking stock counts and reading a static lead_days column, and conflating the two would make Inventory's prompt harder to keep narrow and auditable.
